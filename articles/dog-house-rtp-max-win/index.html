The Dog House RTP and Max Win: What the Paytable Actually Says

The headline 96.51% return and the 6,750x ceiling describe two different things, and neither one describes an evening at the reels. Here is what each number is worth.

By Ryan Murton · Crash games analyst 16 August 2026

🔞 18+ only · Real-money gambling

Two figures follow The Dog House everywhere it is listed: a return to player of 96.51% and a maximum win of 6,750x the stake. Both come from Pragmatic Play's own paytable, and both are routinely read as promises about a session. They are not.

What 96.51% actually measures

Return to player is a long-run average over millions of simulated spins. It is the share of everything staked that the game pays back across that whole sample — not per spin, not per hour, not per hundred spins. On a high-volatility slot the spread around that average is wide enough that a few hundred spins can land almost anywhere.

There is a second catch. Pragmatic Play ships several RTP configurations of the same game and the operator picks one. The number in the game's own info panel is the only one that applies to the site you are playing on — a review quoting 96.51% is quoting the default, not necessarily yours.

Why the max win is the least useful number

6,750x requires the free spins round to line up sticky wilds and multipliers in close to the best arrangement the maths allows. It is real, it is verifiable in the paytable, and it happens rarely enough that no bankroll plan should assume it. Treating it as an outcome you are working toward is how sessions get sized wrong.

The useful reading of a max win is as a description of the shape of the game — where the money is concentrated — not as a goal.

What high volatility costs

  1. Most of the return is parked in the free spins round, so the base game pays back less than the headline figure suggests.

  2. Stretches of 100+ spins with nothing meaningful are ordinary, not a sign of a broken game.

  3. Stake size should be set against that dry run, not against the average.
